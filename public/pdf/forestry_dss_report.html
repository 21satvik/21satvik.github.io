Part 1 — The Decision Problem Context & Scope
Problem Statement

Ireland's Afforestation Gap

Ireland's afforestation challenge is not simply to plant more trees. It is to decide which species should be planted on which soils to deliver the best combined carbon, biodiversity, and financial outcome. With forest cover at 11.8% against an 18% target, and only 8,000 ha planted annually versus 89,500 ha needed, DAFM and landowners need a data-driven DSS to optimise every hectare planted.

11.8% Current Forest Cover
18.0% National Target
447,528 Hectares Gap
8,000 ha/year Current Rate
89,506 ha/year Needed by 2027
11× Required Rate Increase

This is a multi-criteria decision problem involving policy makers, landowners, ecologists, and funding bodies. The Tableau workbook was therefore approached as a Forestry DSS rather than a static dashboard. The analysis focused on user-controlled views: species filters, soil suitability, thinning versus no-thinning, and weighting across finance, environment, and carbon.

Information Requirements

What Decision-Makers Need to Know

Six categories of information were identified as essential for credible afforestation decisions. Each became a KPI theme in the DSS.

1
National Target

Users need the current forest baseline, the 18% target, the remaining hectare gap, and the annual planting rate required to close it. Without this, strategies cannot be judged as realistic or symbolic.

2
Species-to-Soil Matching

A species cannot be recommended on carbon or finance alone if it is unsuitable for the soil. Decision-makers need hectares of suitable land by species and soil type, with transparent translation from UK species preference data into Irish soil classifications.

3
Environment & Biodiversity

The DSS must show more than raw carbon output: wildlife value, debris quality, and native suitability. High-carbon choices can create environmental trade-offs that undermine the legitimacy of large-scale afforestation.

4
Finance & Incentives

Adoption depends on landowner incentives. Users must compare grant and premium outcomes for farmers and non-farmers, and identify which forest type and species combinations give the strongest return per hectare.

5
Temporal Carbon Performance

Cumulative sequestration (tCO2e/ha) must be shown across years 5 to 200, with thinning versus no-thinning comparisons. Carbon leadership changes with time horizon and management regime, so point estimates distort decisions.

6
Optimisation & Trade-offs

No single species dominates across carbon, biodiversity, and finance simultaneously. An integrated ranking mechanism must reveal how recommendations shift when priorities are re-weighted.

Data Cleaning

Preparation & Verification

Cleaning focused on reliability for decision-making, not presentation. The main issues were inconsistent suitability labels, sparse narrative fields, and no shared vocabulary between Irish soil descriptions and UK species terms.

Woodland Carbon Code uses short codes (SS, OK) while DAFM uses full names. A mapping table was built to join them.

Years 150–195
Clear-fell cap and claimable percentage were missing for 8 periods across all 60 configs. Linear interpolation on period_end_yr filled the gap cleanly.

Finance was reconstructed as time-dependent. Grant is one-off; premium accrues annually for 20 years (farmer) or 15 years (non-farmer), then caps.

Part 3 — The DSS Six Dashboards
Dashboard Tour

Six Decision Surfaces

Each dashboard is built around a formal problem statement using the [Who] experiences [what gap] [when/where] framework. The purpose of each is to support a decision, not just describe the problem.

01

Landing Page Overview & Navigation

Problem: DAFM and forestry planners lack a single-entry point to understand the scale of the afforestation challenge before drilling into species and soil decisions.

CSF: Shows the national gap, current cover, target cover, and annual planting shortfall.

Controls: Navigation buttons · BAN cards for cover, target, gap, rate required

02

Government Targets National Progress

Problem: Planners lack a tool to test which species, soils, and planting rates can close the 447,528 ha gap most effectively.

CSF: Suitable hectares by species and soil; annual planting rate needed to close the gap.

Controls: Species filter · Soil suitability filter · Planting-rate slider · Target year selector

03

Environment & Biodiversity Ecological Trade-offs

Problem: Decision-makers lack a quantified view of the ecological trade-offs in species selection, especially native versus non-native.

CSF: Composite biodiversity score; native vs. non-native soil suitability; ecological value per suitable hectare.

Controls: Biodiversity component toggle · Native/non-native filter · Species selector

04

Financial Returns Grant & Premium Income

Problem: Landowners lack a clear financial view of lifetime premium value by species, forest type, and farmer status.

CSF: Farmer and non-farmer premium per hectare; return per euro of government investment.

Controls: Farmer/non-farmer toggle · Species selector · Forest type filter · Carbon price slider

05

Carbon Sequestration Temporal Performance

Problem: Carbon-focused users lack a time-based view of how species choice and management regime affect sequestration.

CSF: Cumulative tCO2e/ha over time; thinning vs. no-thinning effect; species ranking at any year.

Controls: Thinning toggle · Year slider (5–200) · Yield class per species · Spacing per species

06

Optimisation & Trade-off Priority-Weighted Recommendation

Problem: Policy users lack an integrated tool that combines carbon, biodiversity, and finance into one ranked recommendation.

CSF: Composite score across dimensions; sensitivity to weight changes; suggested hectare allocation.

Controls: Four weight sliders (Carbon, Finance, Resilience, Ecology) · Species config parameters · Strategy Sankey · Actual vs Suggested Plan

Scoring Logic

How the Recommendation is Built

Dashboard 6 is the decision engine. Each species is scored across four normalised dimensions, multiplied by user weights, then ranked and converted into a suggested hectare allocation.

C
Carbon (dynamic)

Cumulative tCO2e/ha sequestered at the selected year. Varies by spacing, yield class, management regime, and year horizon. Normalised by dividing by the maximum across all five species. Source: UK Woodland Carbon Code biomass tables.

F
Finance (semi-dynamic)

Cumulative grant plus annual premium per hectare at the selected year. Grant is one-off; premium runs 20 years (farmer) or 15 years (non-farmer), then caps. Normalised same as carbon. Source: DAFM Afforestation Scheme 2023–2027.

R
Resilience (static)

21 minus the sum of seven risk scores (drought, waterlogging, wind, frost, disease, exposure, establishment), each scored 0–3. Higher means more resilient. Fixed per species. Normalised by dividing by 21.

E
Ecology (static)

Sum of Wildlife, Debris Quality, and Biodiversity scores, each 1–5. Maximum 15. Fixed per species. Normalised by dividing by 15.

Part 4 — Findings Species Verdict
Key Insight

No Single Species Wins

Sitka Spruce leads on carbon and available land, but Common Alder performs more strongly when financial return and environmental value are considered. Sessile Oak remains competitive because it combines strong carbon with native ecological value. The best recommendation is a mixed planting regime, not a single-species answer.

Sitka Spruce
Carbon leader, ecology laggard
1,045 tCO2e/ha at year 100 — 38% higher than the next species. But ecology score 4/15 and non-native status create biodiversity trade-offs that weaken its legitimacy as a primary recommendation.
Common Alder
Balanced all-rounder
Ecology 13/15, resilience 11/21, finance €29,584/ha. Performs strongly on every dimension except carbon. The only nitrogen-fixing species and well-suited to riparian Irish soils.
Sessile Oak
Native ecological champion
Perfect 15/15 ecology score, native keystone species supporting 2,300+ associated species. Competitive carbon (758 tCO2e/ha) when given time, but slow to establish.
Scots Pine
Resilience champion
15/21 resilience — highest of any species. Drought-tolerant, windfirm, formerly native. Ideal for marginal upland sites where other species struggle.
Sensitivity

Rankings Shift by Priority

The Strategy Sankey demonstrates that recommendations are entirely weight-dependent. Setting equal weights (25/25/25/25) produces a different ranking than carbon-maximisation (70/10/10/10) or ecology-first (10/10/10/70).

Recommendation

A Mixed Regime, Weighted to Priority

The DSS does not prescribe a single answer. It reveals that afforestation policy must be explicit about its priorities before species allocation can be defended. Three policy postures emerge:

Carbon-led (climate compliance focus): Skew toward Sitka Spruce and Scots Pine on suitable conifer sites. Accept the biodiversity cost but cap non-native share at 50% to preserve ecological legitimacy.

Ecology-led (2027 scheme alignment): Prioritise Sessile Oak and Common Alder on eligible soils. Slower carbon accrual is offset by native keystone status and stronger public acceptance.

Balanced (DSS default): Use equal weights as the baseline policy stance. Suggested allocation favours Sessile Oak (24%) and Common Alder (22%), with Scots Pine (20%) for marginal sites and Sitka Spruce (17%) for carbon-focused parcels.

Limitations

What the Model Does Not Do

Independence Assumption

All four dimensions are treated as independent. In reality, carbon and finance are partially correlated (higher-yielding species earn more carbon credits and often grow in higher-grant forest types).

Site-Agnostic Scores

Risk and ecology are static expert assessments, not site-specific. Actual soil, elevation, and exposure conditions may shift these scores materially.

Scheme Constraints

The model does not enforce DAFM species mixing rules, minimum broadleaf percentages, or scheme eligibility (e.g., forest type restrictions).

Interpolated Data

Carbon data beyond year 150 includes interpolated claimable percentage and clearfell cap values where the WCC tables had gaps. Cumulative sequestration values are direct from source.

Conclusion

A Decision Tool, Not a Report

Unlike traditional business intelligence tools, a good DSS must enhance managerial judgment through predictive modelling, alternative ranking, and iterative learning rather than merely aggregating data (Keen and Scott-Morton, 1978). This Forestry DSS adds value by allowing users to explore trade-offs directly through filters, toggles, and weighting controls. Instead of offering one fixed answer, it supports transparent and adaptable decision-making for planners, policy makers, and landowners through scenario exploration.
