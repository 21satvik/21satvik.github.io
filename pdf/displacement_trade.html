The Displacement Trade How AI Product Releases Move Markets

An event study measuring stock market reactions to AI product launches, an empirical exposure scoring framework, and a forward-looking watchlist.

The Scene

The Claude Crash. Then the Claude Rally.

On January 30, 2026, Anthropic released a legal plugin for Claude Cowork. Contract review workflows and negotiation playbooks, all in open-source markdown files on GitHub. By Tuesday February 3, the market had responded.

Thomson Reuters dropped 16%. Its worst day on record. RELX fell 14%. LegalZoom sank nearly 20%. FactSet dropped over 10%. S&P Global, a company whose credit ratings franchise is protected by regulation, fell 18%. Nine stocks showed statistically significant abnormal returns. $285 billion in market cap gone. Jefferies called it the SaaSpocalypse.

Then, on February 24, Thomson Reuters surged 14%. Its biggest intraday gain in 26 years. CEO Steve Hasker got on stage at Anthropic's own enterprise event. CoCounsel had crossed a million users. Anthropic was suddenly a partner, not a threat.

Same stock. Same AI company behind both moves. Fifteen trading days apart.

That whiplash is what made me want to measure this properly. Not the headlines. Not the panic. The actual, statistically testable market impact of AI product releases on individual stocks.

The Method

Stripping Out the Noise: What Actually Moved?

Thomson Reuters dropped 16% on February 3. But the whole market was down that day. How much of that 16% was the Cowork plugin, and how much was just broad market movement?

To answer that, I used an event study. The idea: for each stock, build a model of what it normally does when the market moves. Then on the event day, compare what actually happened to what the model predicted. The difference is the abnormal return: the stock-specific impact that can't be explained by the market.

For each of the 20 companies in my universe, before each of 9 AI release events, I regressed 200 trading days of returns against the S&P 500. That gives a beta (market sensitivity) and alpha (baseline drift) for each stock. On the event day, I plugged in what the S&P did, got an expected return, and subtracted it from the actual return. The Cumulative Abnormal Return (CAR) sums these daily differences over a five-day window around the event [−1, +3], capturing the full market reaction rather than just the first day.

Statistical significance was tested with t-tests. If a CAR is large relative to how much the stock normally fluctuates, it's unlikely to be random. A t-statistic beyond ±2 means the result is significant at the 5% level. Thomson Reuters came in at t = −5.89. That's not noise.

The Findings

Most AI Launches Don't Move Stocks at All

This was the first surprise. I tested 9 events from ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 through Claude Cowork in February 2026. Most of them produced nothing.

ChatGPT's launch: no significant abnormal returns for any of the stocks people assumed it would destroy. GPT-4: same. OpenAI's o1-preview: nothing. Google AI Overviews overlapped with GPT-4o (one day apart), so the effects can't be separated.

The market only breaks when a specific revenue model gets concretely threatened. Abstract AI capability improvements don't move individual stocks. A plugin that automates contract review and says "legal" on the label does.

Two events broke through. The Chegg earnings call on May 2, 2023, when management admitted ChatGPT was hurting their homework help business (CAR: −56%). And Claude Cowork on February 3, 2026, which produced 9 statistically significant CARs across legal, financial data, and enterprise SaaS.

When It Breaks, It Breaks Blind

Here's every stock in the universe ranked by abnormal return from the Cowork event:

Thomson Reuters at −21.8% is the headline. RELX (−19.1%), FactSet (−19.1%), Morningstar (−18.8%), and S&P Global (−18.2%) formed a tight cluster. Wolters Kluwer (−14.0%), ServiceNow (−13.5%), and Moody's (−12.9%) followed. Salesforce (−9.2%) was significant at the 5% level.

But look at what didn't crash. And what crashed despite having no reason to.

The Floor to Displacement
Chegg: +3.0% during the crash
The most AI-vulnerable company in this universe went positive during the Cowork sell-off. Already down 95% from its 2021 peak, a legal plugin doesn't add marginal damage when the market has already priced in your obsolescence.
The Moat That Didn't Matter
S&P Global: −18.2% (score 1.45/10)
S&P Global's credit ratings franchise has a regulatory moat AI cannot replace. Issuers must use NRSRO-designated agencies. My framework scores it 1.45 out of 10 on AI exposure. It still dropped 18.2%, nearly as much as Thomson Reuters at 5.10/10.
Confounded Result: Upwork
+19.8% on DevDay (Nov 2023)
Upwork's positive CAR around OpenAI DevDay is confounded by strong Q3 earnings reported the next day. This result is excluded from displacement interpretation.
Different Mechanism: DeepSeek
Infrastructure repricing
DeepSeek R1 (January 2025) caused Nvidia to drop 13% as the market questioned whether massive GPU buildouts were necessary. AI got cheaper, not better at replacing services. A different mechanism entirely.

The sell-off was indiscriminate. It hit every company labelled "data" or "legal" regardless of actual vulnerability. The raw price drop on February 3 tells you almost nothing about who is genuinely at risk. To separate signal from sector panic, you need a framework.

The Framework

Scoring AI Exposure: Five Dimensions

I scored each of the 20 companies on five dimensions. Revenue concentration comes directly from SEC 10-K filings: Thomson Reuters derives 45% of EBIT from Legal Professionals, RELX gets 13% from Legal, Wolters Kluwer 16%. These are published numbers. The other four are structured qualitative assessments grounded in what the event study revealed about which companies got hit and why.

DimensionWeightSourceWhat It Captures
Revenue Concentration30%SEC 10-K filings% of revenue/EBIT from AI-exposed segments
Substitutability25%Qualitative (1-10)How much of core product AI can replicate today
Moat Weakness20%Qualitative (1-10)Inverse of proprietary data, switching costs, regulation
Price Sensitivity15%Qualitative (1-10)Whether customers will switch to free AI alternatives
Supplier-Competitor10%Binary (0/1)Company is both customer and competitor of AI provider

That last dimension is the one nobody else is measuring. Thomson Reuters uses Anthropic's models to power CoCounsel. Anthropic then launches a legal plugin that competes with CoCounsel. On February 3, that relationship was a threat. On February 24, it was validation. Companies in this dual role face a structurally different kind of exposure.

The Test

Does the Score Predict the Damage?

A framework is only useful if it predicts something. I regressed exposure scores against actual CARs from two events. The results are mixed. And the mixed result is the most interesting finding in the project.

Validation 1: Chegg Earnings (May 2, 2023)
Slope (β)−4.45 R²0.466 p-value0.0009
Strong Validation
Higher exposure score predicts worse CAR. The framework explains 47% of variation. Across events, general AI vulnerability predicts who gets hurt.
Validation 2: Claude Cowork Plugins (Feb 3, 2026)
Slope (β)+1.38 R²0.180 p-value0.062
Unexpected Direction
Higher exposure predicted less damage. The Cowork sell-off hit a sector, not a score. RELX (score 3.7) fell −19%, same as FactSet (score 5.7). Chegg (score 8.4) went positive. The market sold the "legal data" basket regardless of who was actually defensible.

The framework predicts displacement across events over time. It fails within individual events, where sector panic overrides fundamentals. That's not a bug. That's the finding. General AI exposure determines who is structurally vulnerable. The specific trigger event determines when the sell-off happens and which sector gets hit.

Primary Sources

What Management Says vs What the Market Priced In

The gap between how executives describe AI's impact and how the market reacted on February 3 is where the exposure framework finds its sharpest edge.

Thomson Reuters: Feb 3 vs Feb 24
Worst day, then best day in 26 years
On Feb 3, TRI dropped 16% after the Cowork legal plugin. On Feb 24, TRI surged 14% after CEO Steve Hasker presented at Anthropic's enterprise event and CoCounsel was highlighted on stage. The market cannot decide whether Anthropic is Thomson Reuters' biggest threat or its biggest validator.
Hasker on General-Purpose AI
"Cannot meet this standard"
On the Q4 2025 earnings call (Feb 5, two days after the crash), Hasker argued that general-purpose models cannot deliver outputs needed for high-stakes litigation. He described legal AI workflows as "white space" largely incremental to traditional research. CoCounsel had crossed one million users. Anthropic was listed as a key model provider.
LexisNexis: "Cannot Be Replicated"
RELX down 17% YTD when CEO spoke
In March 2026, LexisNexis CEO Sean Fitzpatrick said their proprietary data cannot be replicated and that every new model puts LexisNexis in a better position. RELX's legal segment grew 7% in 2024 with strong AI-driven tool adoption. The market had already priced in a 14% single-day loss.
RELX CEO: AI Is a Net Benefit
Growth attributed to AI tools
CEO Erik Engstrom described AI as a net benefit, attributing growth to analytics and decision tools. 83% of revenue now comes from electronic products, up from 22% in 2000. The business has already transformed once. The question is whether the market priced in a second disruption management says isn't coming.

The pattern across all three: management frames AI as an accelerant for existing moats. The market prices it as existential threat. For Thomson Reuters (45% EBIT from legal, supplier-competitor score = 1), the risk is real but Feb 3 likely overshot. For RELX (13% EBIT from legal, strong proprietary data moat), the sell-off looks like sector contagion.

Core Finding
AI displacement is real, measurable, and predictable over time. But individual sell-offs are sector panics that ignore fundamentals.
  • The score tells you how bad. The exposure framework predicts AI displacement across events (Chegg validation: R² = 0.47, p = 0.0009). Companies broadly vulnerable to AI suffer more when their sector gets targeted.
  • The trigger tells you when. S&P Global (score 1.45) fell −18.2% on Feb 3, nearly as much as Thomson Reuters (score 5.10) at −21.8%. The market panics by sector, not by score.
  • The gap is the signal. Shutterstock won't crash when a legal plugin launches. It'll crash when the next image model drops. And when it does, Fiverr and Upwork are in the blast radius too.
Limitations

What This Analysis Cannot Claim

The market model removes broad market effects but not sector-specific confounders. The Chegg earnings event is a delayed reaction: ChatGPT launched in November 2022 but the stock didn't crash until May 2023. GPT-4o and Google AI Overviews happened on consecutive days (May 13-14, 2024) and their effects cannot be separated. Exposure scores from 10-K segments are backward-looking. FactSet reports geographically rather than by product, so its revenue concentration is a qualitative estimate. The validation sample is 20 stocks, limiting statistical power.

Event study methodology follows MacKinlay (1997). Market model estimated via OLS over 200-day estimation window with 10-day gap. Abnormal returns computed over [−1, +3] and [−1, +10] event windows. Stock prices from yfinance (adjusted close). Revenue concentration verified against SEC 10-K filings for TRI (45% EBIT from Legal Professionals), RELX (13% adjusted OP from Legal), and WKL (16% revenue from Legal & Regulatory).
