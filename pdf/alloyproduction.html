Executive Summary Section 01
Overview

When policy becomes a cost variable.

Classical alloy blending models minimise raw material cost. They assume a neutral policy environment. That assumption no longer holds. This report extends the standard blending LP to incorporate EU carbon pricing, US Section 232 tariffs, and destination-specific market exposure — implemented and solved in FICO Xpress Mosel across six policy scenarios.

The central finding is that policy shocks raise total delivered cost by 13–38% without changing the underlying material mix. The process has hard technical limits — decarbonisation beyond 10–12% is infeasible regardless of carbon price. Destination strategy turns out to matter almost as much as sourcing strategy.

Context & Model Design Section 02
Problem

From blending problem to policy model

A steel producer must manufacture 500 tonnes of shipbuilding steel from seven available raw materials — each with a known chemical composition, unit cost, and availability limit. The steel must meet composition specifications for carbon, copper, and manganese. This is the classical blending LP.

The classical model is mathematically correct but strategically incomplete. It doesn't account for the carbon cost embedded in each material, the tariff cost of selling to the US, or the fact that different destinations face entirely different cost structures. This model extends the base case in three dimensions:

Extension 1 — Multi-Market
EU + US destinations
Steel is sold to both the EU (57%) and the US (43%). Each destination has fixed contractual demand. The model tracks production allocation and cost exposure by market.
Extension 2 — Carbon Cost
€85/tCO₂ (CBAM)
Each raw material carries an emissions factor (kg CO₂/tonne). The EU ETS/CBAM carbon price is applied as an explicit cost term — embedded emissions are no longer cost-neutral.
Extension 3 — Destination Tariffs
50% Section 232
US tariffs under Section 232 are applied as destination-specific ad valorem mark-ups on raw material costs for steel sold to the US market. Partial exemption rates (0.45–0.48) are modelled in sensitivity scenarios.
Green Premium
+€110/t on Material 3
A low-emission "green iron" grade is available at a premium consistent with green-steel decarbonisation literature. The model tests whether carbon pricing makes this cost-competitive.
Mathematical Formulation Section 03
Model

The algebra of delivered cost

Let M = {1,…,7} be the set of raw materials. Decision variables are x_m ≥ 0 (tonnes of material m used) and Q ≥ 0 (total steel produced).

Effective Unit Cost Construction
Base effective cost c_meff = c_mbase + c_mgreen  ∀m ∈ M
US tariff-inclusive cost c_mUS = c_meff × (1 + τ_m)  ∀m ∈ M
EU carbon-inclusive cost c_mEU = c_meff + e_m × rCBAM  ∀m ∈ M
Blended cost (market-weighted) c̃_m = αUS · c_mUS + αEU · c_mEU  ∀m ∈ M
Objective Function
Minimise total delivered cost min Z = Σm∈M c̃_m · x_m

Mosel Implementation

The model was implemented in FICO Xpress Mosel with a modular architecture — core model logic in a central .mos file, policy parameters read from external data files. This separation enables scenario analysis without modifying the constraint structure.

! Policy-aware alloy blending model — FICO Xpress Mosel
model alloy_policy
  uses "mmxprs"

  ! Read parameters from external data files
  parameters
    BASE_COST_FILE = "base_costs.dat"
    POLICY_FILE    = "policy_params.dat"
  end-parameters

  declarations
    M = 1..7                        ! raw materials
    x: array(M) of mpvar           ! decision variables
    Q: mpvar                           ! total production
    c_tilde: array(M) of real        ! blended effective costs
  end-declarations

  ! Construct blended cost: α_US·c_US + α_EU·c_EU
  forall(m in M)
    c_tilde(m) := alpha_us * c_eff(m) * (1 + tariff(m))
                + alpha_eu * (c_eff(m) + co2(m) * r_cbam)

  ! Objective: minimise total delivered cost
  total_cost := sum(m in M) c_tilde(m) * x(m)
  minimize(total_cost)
Scenario-Based Sensitivity Analysis Section 04
Results

Six scenarios. One material mix.

In all six policy scenarios, the cost-optimal material mix remains unchanged — 80% primary iron, 18.8% secondary alloy, with emissions locked at 956,467 kg CO₂. Policy does not switch technology. It raises the cost of the technology you already use.

Key Insights & Conclusions Section 05
Findings

Three things the model is telling you

01
Policy raises costs without switching technology
Across all six scenarios, the optimal material mix never changes — 80% primary iron, 18.8% secondary alloy. Carbon pricing and tariffs impose cost penalties of 13–38%, but the current process is too constrained by chemical specifications to respond by switching inputs. The response to policy is financial, not operational.
02
Destination strategy matters almost as much as sourcing strategy
The difference between a US-focused and EU-focused sales mix is nearly 8 percentage points in total cost — comparable to the impact of doubling US tariffs. Where you sell is almost as important as what you buy. Market geography is an underappreciated lever in production cost planning.
03
Decarbonisation has a hard technical ceiling
The model achieves feasible solutions at 5% and 10% emissions reductions across all scenarios, but at marginal abatement costs of €1,466–€1,924/tCO₂ — many times higher than the prevailing carbon price. At 12%, every scenario becomes infeasible. This is not a cost problem; it is a materials and chemistry problem. Significant decarbonisation requires new process routes, not just optimised blending.
Strategic Implications
What this means for a steel producer operating in both markets.
  • Build policy resilience plans, not process efficiency plans. Current carbon pricing and tariffs raise costs by up to 38% without creating any incentive to change the material mix. The process is too constrained. The firm needs financial buffers and destination flexibility, not incremental input substitution.
  • Treat destination mix as a strategic variable. An 8 percentage point cost difference between EU-centric and US-centric sales is large enough to justify active market portfolio management — not just passive fulfilment of whichever orders arrive.
  • Plan for the 10–12% hard ceiling explicitly. Any decarbonisation target above 10% cannot be achieved by optimising the existing blend. Communicating this boundary to stakeholders is itself a value of the modelling exercise — it tells you where engineering investment needs to precede policy compliance.
