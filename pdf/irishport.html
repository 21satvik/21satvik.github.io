Executive Summary Section 01
Overview

Resilient in some ways. Quietly fragile in others.

More than 90% of Ireland's trade by volume moves through its ports. This analysis examines CSO data covering quarterly freight activity at eight Irish commercial ports from 2017 to 2025 — using descriptive statistics, hypothesis testing, ANOVA, and regression to assess port dominance, trade balance, seasonality, and resilience to major shocks.

The picture that emerges is of a trade network that bounced back from a global pandemic without breaking a sweat, but is still absorbing the quieter, slower damage done by Brexit — and carrying a structural concentration risk that won't self-correct without deliberate intervention.

Inferential Statistics Section 03
Hypothesis Testing

What the data actually confirms

Five inferential tests were conducted to formally evaluate the hypotheses suggested by the descriptive analysis. All tests use α = 0.05 as the significance threshold.

Test 1 — Imports vs Exports

Two-sample pooled t-test (one-tailed) Reject H₀
H₀ μ_exports = μ_imports — no difference in mean tonnage H₁ μ_exports < μ_imports — exports handled in lower volumes n 31 observations per group · pooled variance (ratio < 2) Result t = −3.36  ·  df = 60  ·  p = 0.001 (one-tailed)  ·  critical value = −1.67
✓ Reject H₀ — statistically significant at α = 0.05
Imports are processed in significantly larger volumes than exports. This structural imbalance — confirmed at p = 0.001 — means Ireland's port infrastructure is optimised to receive goods, not dispatch them. Supply chain shocks that disrupt inbound flows hit Irish ports harder than equivalent disruptions to outbound trade.

Test 2 — Pre-COVID vs Post-COVID

Paired t-test (one-tailed) · 2017–2019 vs 2022–2024 Fail to Reject H₀
H₀ μ_pre = μ_post — no difference in mean tonnage pre- vs post-COVID H₁ μ_pre > μ_post — pre-COVID tonnage was higher n 31 paired quarterly observations Result t = 0.05  ·  df = 30  ·  p = 0.48 (one-tailed)  ·  critical value = 1.70
— Fail to Reject H₀ — no statistically significant decline
Irish ports recovered to pre-pandemic levels without detectable structural damage. The slight dip visible in the data falls within normal variation. COVID-19, for all the disruption it caused elsewhere, did not reshape Ireland's port throughput — a genuine operational achievement.

Test 3 — Pre-Brexit vs Post-Brexit (GB/NI tonnage)

Paired t-test (one-tailed) · GB/NI quarterly tonnage only Reject H₀
H₀ μ_pre = μ_post — no change in quarterly GB/NI tonnage before and after Brexit H₁ μ_pre > μ_post — post-Brexit tonnage with GB/NI is lower n 31 paired quarterly observations of GB/NI freight Result t = 3.39  ·  df = 30  ·  p = 0.001 (one-tailed)  ·  critical value = 1.70
✗ Reject H₀ — highly significant decline confirmed
Brexit produced a statistically significant and sustained reduction in Irish maritime trade with Great Britain and Northern Ireland. New customs procedures and regulatory friction made UK-linked routes less attractive. The land bridge through Britain — once the default route for Irish goods heading to Europe — is being replaced by direct continental routes. The shift is already underway in the data.

Test 4 — Seasonality: ANOVA Across Quarters

One-way ANOVA · Q1 vs Q2 vs Q3 vs Q4 Fail to Reject H₀
H₀ μ_Q1 = μ_Q2 = μ_Q3 = μ_Q4 — no difference in mean tonnage across quarters H₁ At least one quarter exhibits a different mean tonnage n 31 observations per quarter Result F = 0.08  ·  df_between = 3, df_within = 169  ·  p = 0.971  ·  critical = 2.70 SS SSTR = 64,056,919  ·  SSE = 44,844,307,003  ·  MSTR = 21,352,306  ·  MSE = 265,350,929
— Fail to Reject H₀ — no meaningful seasonality detected
Despite Q4 showing slightly higher descriptive averages, the ANOVA finds this difference is indistinguishable from normal operational variation (p = 0.971). Irish port activity does not follow a seasonal rhythm. For port operators, this simplifies resource planning — there is no statistical basis for dramatically different staffing or berth allocation by quarter.

Test 5 — Port Differences: ANOVA Across the Four Largest Ports

One-way ANOVA · Dublin vs Cork vs Shannon Foynes vs Waterford Reject H₀
H₀ μ_Dublin = μ_Cork = μ_Shannon = μ_Waterford — no difference in mean quarterly tonnage H₁ At least one port handles significantly different mean quarterly tonnage n 31 observations per port Result F = 16.36  ·  df_between = 3, df_within = 116  ·  p ≈ 0.000  ·  critical = 2.68 SS SSTR = 16,188,371  ·  SSE = 38,251,380  ·  MSTR = 5,396,124  ·  MSE = 329,753
✓ Reject H₀ — highly significant, p ≈ 0.000
Dublin's dominance is not a descriptive illusion — it is statistically confirmed at the highest level of significance. The differences between ports are real, large, and consistent over time. This concentration of national freight in a single hub represents a systemic risk: any disruption at Dublin is a national trade event.

Conclusions & Recommendations Section 04
Summary

What the data is saying

Ireland's port system survived a global pandemic without structural damage. That is a genuine achievement. But it carries three unresolved vulnerabilities that won't fix themselves.

01
Single-hub concentration creates national-level risk
Dublin handles 50% of national tonnage, confirmed at p ≈ 0.000. Its dominance is stable — neither growing nor declining. Any disruption at Dublin is not a local event — it is a national trade event. The regression model (R² = 0.67) provides a solid baseline for ongoing monitoring.
02
Import dependence exposes Ireland to global supply shocks
Imports run at nearly 2× export volumes, confirmed at p = 0.001. Ireland's high-value export industries are weight-light — pharmaceuticals and technology don't strain port infrastructure the way bulk imports do. But when global shipping seizes up, import-heavy ports feel it first.
03
Brexit rewired Ireland's trade geography — permanently
Quarterly trade with GB/NI has fallen by a statistically confirmed 245,000–1,195,000 tonnes (95% CI). COVID didn't do this — it recovered. Brexit didn't recover. The data confirms the reorientation toward direct EU routes is underway, but the transition is incomplete.
Strategic Recommendations
Four policy interventions the data supports.
  • Invest in secondary ports. Shannon Foynes and Cork need sustained capacity development to reduce systemic dependency on Dublin. The concentration is stable — it won't self-correct without deliberate intervention.
  • Support export infrastructure. Develop targeted export incentives, logistics support, and direct shipping routes to reduce the chronic 2:1 import imbalance. The gap is structural and persistent.
  • Accelerate direct EU maritime links. The shift away from the GB land bridge is rational and data-supported. Invest in making it permanent rather than leaving it partially complete.
  • Smooth operational planning across quarters. No statistically significant seasonality exists. Quarter-specific resource allocation is not data-justified — planning can be smoothed across the year without efficiency loss.
